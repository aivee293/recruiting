# Hiring Decision Frameworks

Frameworks for moving from aggregated interview feedback to a final hiring decision. Choose the model that fits your org's decision-making culture and the criticality of the role.

## Weighted Scorecard Method

The default framework for most hiring decisions. Structured, transparent, and minimizes bias.

### How It Works

1. Define the competencies being assessed (from the job brief or persona)
2. Classify each competency as must-have (weight: 3x) or nice-to-have (weight: 1x)
3. Each interviewer rates the candidate on their assigned competencies (1-4 scale)
4. Aggregate ratings per competency — flag disagreements rather than averaging them
5. Multiply each aggregated rating by its weight
6. Sum weighted scores for a total
7. Use the total as a conversation starter, not a final answer

### When to Use

- Standard IC and management hires
- Roles with clearly defined competency requirements
- When you need a defensible, structured process
- When multiple candidates are being compared

### Strengths

- Forces explicit prioritization of what matters
- Makes the decision criteria transparent to everyone
- Creates a paper trail that supports fair hiring practices
- Reduces the influence of a single loud voice in the debrief

### Weaknesses

- Can create false precision — a score of 27 vs 26 is not meaningfully different
- Requires well-defined competencies upfront (garbage in, garbage out)
- Can feel mechanical if followed too rigidly
- Doesn't capture intangibles well

### Implementation Steps

1. During intake, define 4-6 must-have and 2-4 nice-to-have competencies with the hiring manager
2. Map each competency to an interview stage and interviewer
3. Build a scorecard template with the competencies, weights, and rating scale
4. After all interviews, aggregate into the comparison matrix
5. Present the matrix at the debrief as a discussion tool, not a verdict

## Bar Raiser Approach

Adapted from Amazon's hiring methodology. Adds an independent evaluator to prevent bar erosion over time.

### How It Works

- A trained interviewer from outside the hiring team participates in the interview loop
- This person (the "bar raiser") has veto power over the hiring decision
- The bar raiser evaluates both the candidate AND the quality of the interview process
- The core question: "Would this person raise the average performance level of the team?"

### Purpose

- Prevent the "warm body" hire — when urgency overrides quality
- Maintain consistent hiring standards across teams and managers
- Provide an outside perspective free from team-specific biases or desperation
- Catch process failures (poorly designed questions, untested competencies)

### When to Use

- Scaling organizations where hiring volume creates bar erosion risk
- Teams that have made regretted hires recently
- Senior or critical roles where a bad hire has outsized impact
- When the hiring manager has limited hiring experience

### The Bar Raiser's Checklist

- Were all must-have competencies tested with sufficient depth?
- Is there concrete behavioral evidence for every "hire" recommendation?
- Would I want this person on my own team?
- Am I seeing urgency bias from the hiring manager ("we just need someone")?
- Does this candidate raise the average, or are we settling?

### Strengths

- Prevents gradual bar erosion that teams don't notice internally
- Adds accountability — someone with no stake in the outcome evaluates objectively
- Improves interview quality over time (bar raisers coach interviewers)

### Weaknesses

- Requires a pool of trained bar raisers (investment to build)
- Can slow down hiring if bar raisers are bottlenecked
- Risk of bar raisers becoming gatekeepers rather than quality checks
- Doesn't work well in very small organizations (< 30 people)

## Portfolio Hiring

Hiring for team composition rather than individual excellence. The right hire depends on what the team already has.

### How It Works

Instead of asking "Is this the best candidate?", ask "Is this the best candidate *for what the team needs right now*?"

1. Map the current team's strengths and gaps (skills, experience, working styles, perspectives)
2. Identify what the team is missing — not just technical skills, but collaboration patterns, domain knowledge, seniority mix
3. Evaluate candidates against the gap, not just against the role description
4. A candidate who fills a critical team gap may be more valuable than a "better" candidate who duplicates existing strengths

### Complementary Hire vs Best Athlete

| Approach | When to Use | Example |
|----------|------------|---------|
| **Complementary** | Team has specific gaps, needs balance | Team of senior ICs needs someone who can handle stakeholder communication |
| **Best Athlete** | Greenfield team, unclear needs, high-growth | Early-stage startup hiring generalists |

### How to Assess for Portfolio Fit

- Ask the hiring manager: "If you could change one thing about your team's capabilities, what would it be?"
- Review the team roster: seniority distribution, tenure, skill overlaps, diversity of perspective
- During interviews, probe for the specific gap: "How do you approach [gap area]?"
- Weight the gap competency higher in the scorecard

### Strengths

- Produces better-performing teams (diverse skills > uniform excellence)
- Helps break the "hire people like us" pattern
- Addresses real team needs rather than abstract role requirements

### Weaknesses

- Harder to explain to candidates why they weren't selected ("you were qualified, but not what we needed")
- Requires honest team assessment (managers may not acknowledge gaps)
- Can be misused to justify a predetermined choice

## Consensus vs Veto Models

How the interview panel's input translates into a final decision.

### Consensus Model

**How it works:** All interviewers must agree on a "Hire" recommendation.

| Pros | Cons |
|------|------|
| High bar — only candidates everyone believes in get through | Slow — one holdout blocks the decision |
| Surfaces concerns that might be dismissed in other models | Tends toward false negatives (good candidates rejected) |
| Every interviewer feels their voice matters | Pressure to conform — quiet disagreement gets suppressed |

**Best for:** Small teams, critical hires, roles where a bad hire is very costly.

### Majority Model

**How it works:** Majority of interviewers recommend "Hire". Typically 3/4 or 4/5.

| Pros | Cons |
|------|------|
| Faster than consensus | Can override a valid concern from the minority |
| Balances speed and rigor | May miss red flags that only one interviewer caught |
| Handles the "impossible to please everyone" reality | Interviewers in the minority may disengage from future loops |

**Best for:** Mid-volume hiring, roles where speed matters, experienced interview panels.

### Veto Model

**How it works:** Any single interviewer can block a hire (typically limited to must-have competency failures).

| Pros | Cons |
|------|------|
| Catches red flags — one strong "no" is enough | High false-negative rate — one bad interviewer experience kills the candidacy |
| Maintains a high bar | Can be weaponized by interviewers with personal biases |
| Simple to implement | Discourages risk-taking on non-traditional candidates |

**Best for:** Senior/leadership hires, bar-raiser processes, roles where a bad hire has outsized negative impact.

### HM Decision with Input (Recommended Default)

**How it works:** The hiring manager makes the final call after hearing all interviewer input. Interviewers advise; HM decides.

| Pros | Cons |
|------|------|
| Clear accountability — one person owns the decision | HM can ignore feedback (but it's documented) |
| Faster — no need for full consensus | Risk of HM bias if they override without justification |
| Interviewers contribute without the pressure of deciding | Panel may feel their input doesn't matter if overridden often |
| Most natural fit for how organizations actually work | Requires a disciplined HM who takes feedback seriously |

**Best for:** Most hiring. Pair with a veto override for must-have competency failures.

**Recommended implementation:**
- Default to HM Decision with Input
- Reserve veto power for must-have competency failures only (not nice-to-haves, not "vibes")
- If HM overrides a concern, document the rationale
- Track outcomes — if overridden concerns consistently predict bad hires, revisit the model

## When to Pass Despite Good Scores

Not every qualified candidate should be hired. Watch for these patterns:

- **All nice-to-haves strong but a must-have is borderline** — The scorecard total looks good, but the weighted must-have score reveals a gap. Don't let nice-to-have strength compensate for a must-have weakness.
- **Strong interview performance but references raise concerns** — Some candidates are excellent interviewers but the signal from people who've worked with them tells a different story. Take reference concerns seriously, especially patterns across multiple references.
- **Strong individual contributor signals but team dynamics concern** — If multiple interviewers note collaboration concerns independently, this is a pattern worth heeding — especially for roles that require heavy cross-functional work.
- **Overqualified and likely to leave in 6-12 months** — A strong candidate who sees the role as a stepping stone creates a false positive. Hiring and onboarding costs are real. Probe motivations and timeline honestly.
- **"Hire by default" — no strong signal in any direction** — A candidate who doesn't generate any strong opinions (positive or negative) is often a sign of insufficient assessment, not of an average candidate. Dig deeper before deciding.

## When to Hire Despite Concerns

Sometimes the right decision is to hire with eyes open rather than wait for perfection:

- **Concern is about a nice-to-have, not a must-have** — If all must-haves are met and the gap is in a nice-to-have, that's what development plans are for. Don't let perfect be the enemy of good.
- **Concern is coachable and developable** — The candidate lacks something that can be taught in 3-6 months, and you have the infrastructure to teach it. Document the development plan as a condition of the hire.
- **Market conditions make waiting unrealistic** — In a tight market, the candidate who's 85% of what you need today may be better than the candidate who's 100% but arrives 6 months later (or never). Be honest about this trade-off.
- **Candidate brings a rare combination that outweighs the gap** — Sometimes a candidate's unique strengths create more value than the gap costs. Assess this explicitly rather than hand-waving.
- **The concern is based on thin evidence** — If only one interviewer flagged the concern and they can't articulate specific behavioral evidence, the concern may be bias rather than signal. Probe before letting it block the hire.
