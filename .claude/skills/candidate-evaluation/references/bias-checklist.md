# Bias Checklist for Candidate Evaluation

Practical checklists for recruiters and hiring managers to use before, during, and after hiring debriefs. Print these out or share them in the debrief meeting.

## Pre-Debrief Checklist (For the Debrief Facilitator)

Review these questions before you walk into the debrief room.

- [ ] **Have all scorecards been submitted?** Do not proceed with missing scorecards. Late submissions get influenced by the debrief discussion, which defeats the purpose of independent evaluation.
- [ ] **Is the evidence concrete or vague?** Scan each scorecard for specifics. Flag any assessment that says "seemed strong" or "didn't feel right" without a behavioral example. You'll need to press for specifics in the debrief.
- [ ] **Are any interviewers rating based on gut feeling rather than observed behavior?** Look for scorecards that give a rating but don't cite what the candidate actually said or did.
- [ ] **Have I identified the areas of agreement and disagreement?** Prepare the agenda around disagreements — consensus areas need less airtime.
- [ ] **Are there pattern concerns across candidates?** If diverse candidates are consistently rated lower on subjective criteria ("communication style", "culture fit", "executive presence"), flag this for discussion.
- [ ] **Has any interviewer been influenced by another before submitting?** Check timestamps — if two scorecards were submitted minutes apart after a hallway conversation, the second one may be contaminated.
- [ ] **Am I comparing candidates to the role requirements or to each other?** Re-read the must-have competencies before the debrief. The bar is the bar, regardless of how many candidates you've seen.
- [ ] **Is the hiring manager's mind already made up?** If the HM has expressed a strong preference before the debrief, plan to ensure other voices are heard first. The debrief should inform the decision, not rubber-stamp it.
- [ ] **Have all must-have competencies been assessed?** Check the interview plan against the scorecards. If a must-have was supposed to be tested in Stage 3 but the interviewer didn't cover it, you have a gap — not a pass.
- [ ] **Am I prepared to facilitate, not advocate?** Your role is to surface the best decision, not push for a specific outcome. If you have a strong opinion, share it as one input among many.
- [ ] **Do I have the comparison matrix ready?** Prepare the weighted scorecard before the meeting so discussion can focus on evidence, not arithmetic.
- [ ] **Have I set the speaking order?** Plan for junior interviewers to speak first on each competency to prevent anchoring by senior voices.

## During-Debrief Checklist (Share in Meeting)

Read these aloud at the start of the debrief, or display them where everyone can see.

- [ ] **"Are we comparing this candidate to the role requirements, or to other candidates?"** — The bar is defined by the job, not by whoever we interviewed yesterday. A candidate is a hire or not regardless of who else is in the pipeline.
- [ ] **"What specific behavior did you observe?"** — Ask this every time someone gives a rating without evidence. "They were strong on system design" is not evidence. "They decomposed the problem into three services, identified the bottleneck, and proposed a caching strategy with trade-offs" is evidence.
- [ ] **"Would we have the same concern if the candidate were [different demographic]?"** — This is uncomfortable to ask but necessary. If someone flags "communication style" or "executive presence", test whether the concern would exist for a candidate from a different background.
- [ ] **"Is this a pattern or a single data point?"** — One awkward answer in a 45-minute interview is a data point. The same issue surfacing across three interviews is a pattern. Treat them differently.
- [ ] **"Are we weighting this concern correctly — is it a must-have or a nice-to-have?"** — Concerns about nice-to-haves should not block a hire. If the team is treating a nice-to-have as a must-have, recalibrate.
- [ ] **"Who hasn't spoken yet?"** — Silence doesn't mean agreement. Directly invite quieter panelists to share their perspective before moving to a decision.
- [ ] **"Are we holding this candidate to the same standard as previous hires?"** — If the team hired someone 6 months ago who had a similar gap, consistency matters. Either the bar has changed (document why) or we're applying it unevenly.
- [ ] **"Is our concern based on what happened in the interview, or on an assumption about what would happen on the job?"** — Interview performance and job performance are correlated but not identical. Be explicit about which one you're evaluating.
- [ ] **"Let's separate what's coachable from what's not."** — Some gaps close with onboarding and mentorship. Others are fundamental. Don't treat them the same way.
- [ ] **"What would change our mind?"** — If someone is firmly in the "no hire" camp, ask what evidence would shift their position. If nothing would change their mind, that's a sign the assessment may not be evidence-based.

## Post-Debrief Checklist (For the Recruiter)

Reflect on these questions within 24 hours of the debrief.

- [ ] **Was the process fair?** Did every interviewer have a chance to share their perspective? Were any voices dismissed or talked over? Did seniority or role authority disproportionately influence the outcome?
- [ ] **Were all voices heard?** Did junior interviewers speak before senior ones? Did anyone seem to change their position to match the room rather than based on new evidence?
- [ ] **Did we make an evidence-based decision?** Can I point to specific behavioral evidence for the final recommendation, or was the decision driven by gut feeling, likeability, or pattern-matching?
- [ ] **Were there any moments where bias may have influenced the discussion?** Note them for future process improvement — not to relitigate the decision, but to improve the next debrief.
- [ ] **Are there process improvements for next time?** Was a competency undertested? Did an interviewer need coaching on scorecard quality? Should the interview plan be adjusted?
- [ ] **Did I document the decision rationale?** Write down why the candidate was hired, passed on, or moved to additional assessment. Future-you (and future-hiring-managers) will thank you.
- [ ] **For rejected candidates — is there a silver medalist opportunity?** If the candidate was qualified but not selected, flag them for future roles rather than losing them entirely.

## Common Bias Scenarios with Facilitator Responses

Real situations that come up in debriefs and how to handle them.

### 1. "I just didn't get a good vibe from them."

**What's happening:** The interviewer is relying on intuition rather than behavioral evidence. This can be legitimate pattern recognition, but it can also be bias.

**Facilitator response:** "I hear you — can you help me pinpoint what drove that feeling? Was there a specific moment in the interview, something they said or did, that triggered it? I want to make sure we're capturing the right signal."

### 2. "They're not a culture fit."

**What's happening:** "Culture fit" without specifics is often a proxy for "they're not like us." It can mask similarity bias, demographic bias, or simple unfamiliarity.

**Facilitator response:** "When you say culture fit, which specific value or behavior are you referring to? Can you give me an example of what you observed that concerns you? We want to make sure we're evaluating culture add, not culture clone."

### 3. "Compared to Candidate A, Candidate B was much weaker."

**What's happening:** Contrast effect — evaluating candidates relative to each other instead of against the role requirements.

**Facilitator response:** "Let's step back from the comparison for a moment. Does Candidate B meet the bar for our must-have competencies? Let's evaluate them against the role requirements first, and then we can compare."

### 4. "I know they struggled on the technical question, but they were so articulate and impressive overall."

**What's happening:** Halo effect — one strong impression (communication skills) is masking a genuine technical gap.

**Facilitator response:** "Their communication was strong — agreed. But let's look at the technical competency independently. Is [technical skill] a must-have for this role? If so, do we have sufficient evidence that they meet the bar, separate from how well they presented?"

### 5. "They took too long to answer — I think they'd be slow in the role."

**What's happening:** Possible cultural bias (some cultures value thoughtful pauses), introversion bias, or conflating interview pacing with work performance.

**Facilitator response:** "Did the quality of the answer concern you, or the speed? In the actual role, would they have time to think through problems, or is rapid-fire response genuinely required? Let's focus on the substance of what they said."

### 6. "Everyone else said hire, so I'll go along with it."

**What's happening:** Conformity pressure — the interviewer is suppressing a genuine concern to avoid conflict.

**Facilitator response:** "I appreciate the team spirit, but your independent assessment is valuable. If you had a concern, I'd rather hear it now than discover it after the hire. What was your honest read before you heard the group?"

### 7. "They went to [prestigious school] / worked at [top company], so I'm confident they can do the job."

**What's happening:** Credential bias — using pedigree as a shortcut for competency assessment rather than evaluating actual demonstrated skills.

**Facilitator response:** "Strong background for sure. But what did they actually demonstrate in the interview? Did we see evidence of [must-have competency] in their answers, or are we inferring it from their resume?"

### 8. "I've interviewed 15 people for this role and none of them are good enough."

**What's happening:** Possibly a well-calibrated bar, but possibly also: unrealistic expectations, a poorly defined role, or a bar that doesn't match market reality.

**Facilitator response:** "That's a lot of candidates. Let's step back — are we calibrated on what 'good enough' looks like for this role at this compensation level? Should we revisit the must-haves with the hiring manager to make sure we're not screening for a unicorn?"

### 9. "Their English wasn't perfect, so I'm worried about communication with the team."

**What's happening:** Potential language/national origin bias. Communication skills matter, but fluency thresholds should be role-relevant, not perfectionist.

**Facilitator response:** "Can you separate language fluency from communication effectiveness? Did they convey their ideas clearly, structure their thoughts logically, and respond to follow-up questions? For this role, what level of language proficiency is actually required day-to-day?"

### 10. "They asked a lot of questions about work-life balance — I'm not sure they're committed enough."

**What's happening:** Bias against candidates who set boundaries. Asking about work-life balance is a reasonable due diligence question, not a red flag.

**Facilitator response:** "Asking about working conditions is standard candidate due diligence. Did you see evidence of commitment in other parts of the interview — how they talked about past projects, their career investment, their engagement with the problem? Let's evaluate commitment based on what they've done, not what they've asked."
