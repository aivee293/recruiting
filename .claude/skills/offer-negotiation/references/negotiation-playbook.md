# Negotiation Playbook

Scenario-based scripts for common offer negotiation situations. Each scenario covers the situation, underlying motivation, response strategy, example language, and mistakes to avoid.

---

## 1. Candidate Wants More Base Salary (Above Approved Range)

**Situation:** Candidate says their base expectation is 10-20% above the approved range ceiling. They may cite their current comp, market data, or a competing offer.

**Why this happens:** Base salary feels like the most "real" number. Candidates anchor on it because it compounds into future raises and is the easiest to compare. Some candidates also test whether the initial offer is the real ceiling.

**Response strategy:**

1. Acknowledge the ask without reacting negatively: "I appreciate you being direct about what you need."
2. Reframe to total compensation: "Let me walk you through the full picture, because the base is one piece."
3. Explore what's driving the number: "Is the base itself the priority, or is it that the total package needs to be at a certain level?"
4. If base is truly capped, offer alternatives: sign-on bonus, guaranteed year-1 bonus, accelerated review, equity uplift, additional PTO.
5. If there's room, move in small decreasing increments (e.g., first move is $8K, second is $3K) to signal you're approaching the ceiling.
6. Link your concession: "If we can get to $X, are you ready to sign this week?"

**Example language:**

- "Our range for this level caps at $[X]. I can't move the base beyond that without changing the level, which wouldn't be the right thing for your long-term career path here. What I can do is..."
- "I hear you on base. Let me see what flexibility I have on the sign-on side to close that gap without resetting your base expectations for future comp cycles."
- "If I could bridge the $15K difference with a $15K sign-on and a guaranteed 15% bonus in year 1, would that work?"

**Mistakes to avoid:**

- Immediately saying "let me go back and ask" — this signals there's always more money, and they'll keep pushing.
- Explaining why the range exists (bands, internal equity) in a way that sounds like a lecture. Keep it brief.
- Caving on base without getting a commitment in return. Every concession should be linked to a close.
- Negotiating against yourself by offering more before they've responded to your last position.

## 2. Candidate Has a Competing Offer

**Situation:** Candidate tells you they have an offer from [Competitor] and it's higher / more attractive. They may share details or keep it vague.

**Why this happens:** Sometimes genuine leverage, sometimes a negotiation tactic. Even when real, the competing offer is rarely the only factor — they're telling you because they want you to compete.

**Response strategy:**

1. Stay calm. "That's not surprising — someone with your background should have options."
2. Get specifics: "Can you share the details so I can give you a fair comparison? Base, bonus, equity, and the full picture?"
3. If they won't share: "I understand. Can you tell me what the gap is between our offer and theirs, so I know what I'm working with?"
4. Don't compete dollar-for-dollar on base alone. Compete on total value: comp + role scope + team + growth trajectory.
5. Highlight differentiators the other company can't match: specific projects, team culture, career path, manager quality.
6. If the competing offer is genuinely stronger and you can't match it, acknowledge that honestly. Sometimes the best move is to say: "If this is purely a comp decision, I may not be able to win that fight. But if [specific reasons] matter to you, we're the better long-term bet."
7. Create time pressure: "I want to make sure we put our best foot forward. Can we schedule 30 minutes tomorrow so I can walk you through an updated package?"

**Example language:**

- "I'm glad you're being transparent. Let me compare apples to apples. What does their total Year 1 look like when you add everything up?"
- "We see [Competitor] make aggressive offers to close. What I'd ask you to think about is the 3-year picture — their attrition rate, promotion velocity, and what the role looks like after the honeymoon period."
- "If I can match their total comp, is that what it takes? Or is there something else about their offer that's pulling you?"

**Mistakes to avoid:**

- Panic-matching without understanding the full competing package. You may be competing against a number that includes components they're double-counting.
- Bad-mouthing the competitor. The candidate chose to interview with both of you for a reason.
- Assuming the competing offer is fake. Treat it as real and verify through questions.
- Focusing only on comp when the real draw might be the role, the manager, or the brand.

## 3. Candidate Wants a Different Title (One Level Up)

**Situation:** Candidate says they want Senior Staff instead of Staff, or Director instead of Senior Manager. They feel the title doesn't reflect their experience or market standing.

**Why this happens:** Title is identity. Candidates care about title for external credibility, LinkedIn profile, future job searches, and sometimes internal respect. In some cultures and industries, title carries even more weight than comp.

**Response strategy:**

1. Take it seriously — don't dismiss title as "just a word."
2. Understand the why: "Help me understand what the title means to you. Is it about external positioning, internal authority, or something else?"
3. Check internal feasibility: Does the higher title come with a different comp band? Does it set expectations for scope that don't match the role?
4. If you can do it without changing the band or scope: this is one of the cheapest concessions you can make. Use it.
5. If you can't: explain what the title maps to internally. "At [Company], Director means X scope and Y headcount. The role we're hiring for is genuinely at the Senior Manager scope, and I'd rather set you up for a fast promotion to Director than start you in a title where expectations don't match the role."
6. Offer a path: "Here's what the promotion timeline to [desired title] looks like, and here's what you'd need to demonstrate."

**Example language:**

- "I completely understand why the title matters. Let me check whether we have flexibility. If we can make it work without changing the role's scope, I'd love to."
- "Our leveling framework maps Director to [specific scope]. The role you'd be stepping into is [slightly different scope]. I don't want to set you up with a title that creates misaligned expectations on day one. What I can offer is a 6-month accelerated review with a clear path to Director."
- "If I get you the Director title, would the rest of the package work for you?"

**Mistakes to avoid:**

- Granting a title that doesn't match the role scope — this creates problems on day one with peers and reports.
- Dismissing the request as vanity. Title matters to careers.
- Not checking the downstream effects: does a higher title change the comp band minimum? Will it create compression with existing team members?

## 4. Candidate Wants Relocation Support (Company Doesn't Usually Provide It)

**Situation:** Candidate needs to relocate for the role and asks for relocation assistance. Your company either doesn't have a relo policy or doesn't typically offer it at this level.

**Why this happens:** Relocation is expensive and disruptive. The candidate is asking you to share the risk and cost of a major life change. It's a reasonable ask, even if your policy doesn't cover it.

**Response strategy:**

1. Acknowledge the ask: "Relocation is a big deal, and I want to make sure we set you up for success."
2. Check what's actually possible — even without a formal policy, a one-time lump sum is often approvable as a sign-on variant.
3. Frame a lump sum: "We don't have a managed relocation program, but I can include a $[X] relocation stipend as part of your sign-on. That gives you flexibility to use it however works best for your situation."
4. If the company truly can't offer anything: be honest, then offset with other flexibility. "What if we gave you a flexible start date so you can manage the move on your timeline, plus 2 weeks of temporary housing/hotel covered?"
5. Quantify the value of what you can offer: tax-adjusted lump sum, temporary housing, flexible start, remote work for the first month.

**Example language:**

- "I want to find a way to help with the move. Let me see what I can package as a relocation lump sum. Would $[X] make the move manageable?"
- "We can't do a full managed relocation, but here's what I can offer: $[X] lump sum, plus you can start remote for the first 30 days to give yourself time to settle."
- "What would it take for the relocation piece to not be a dealbreaker? Give me a number and I'll see what I can do."

**Mistakes to avoid:**

- Flatly saying "we don't do relocation" without exploring alternatives.
- Offering managed relocation when a lump sum would be simpler and cheaper.
- Forgetting the tax implications — lump sums are taxed as income. Gross up if possible.
- Not discussing relocation until the offer stage. Surface it early so it's not a surprise.

## 5. Candidate Wants a Delayed Start Date (3+ Months Out)

**Situation:** Candidate asks to start 3+ months from offer acceptance. Reasons vary: notice period, garden leave, planned vacation, current project completion, personal reasons.

**Why this happens:** Long notice periods (common in Europe and senior roles), pre-planned commitments, or wanting a break between jobs. Sometimes a stalling tactic if they're unsure.

**Response strategy:**

1. Understand the reason: "Can you help me understand what's driving the timeline? Is it a notice period, or something else?"
2. If it's a notice period: this is non-negotiable. Respect it. Ask if there's a way to shorten it (some companies allow buyout of notice periods).
3. If it's a planned vacation: accommodate it. "Take the trip. We'll set your start date for when you're back and refreshed."
4. If you suspect stalling: test with a trial close. "If we align on the package today, would you be comfortable signing with a start date of [proposed date]?"
5. Keep them warm during the gap: send team updates, invite to optional team events, connect them with their future manager for casual check-ins.
6. If the delay is genuinely too long for the business: be transparent. "The team is scaling for Q2 and we need someone in seat by [date]. Is there any way to make that work? We could explore a part-time or consulting arrangement for the overlap."

**Example language:**

- "I completely respect the notice period. Let's lock in [date] and I'll make sure the team is ready for your first day."
- "Three months is longer than we'd hoped, but I'd rather wait for the right person than rush. Let's set the start date and keep you connected in the meantime."
- "What if we structured a short consulting engagement to start before your full-time date? That way you're building context and the team gets your input earlier."

**Mistakes to avoid:**

- Pressuring a candidate to break a notice period commitment — this damages trust and signals you don't respect contracts.
- Not keeping the candidate engaged during a long gap — this is when counter-offers and cold feet happen.
- Agreeing to the delay without checking with the hiring manager. Three months of empty seat has a real cost.

## 6. Candidate's Current Employer Counters (Significant Raise)

**Situation:** Candidate accepted your offer (or is about to) and their current employer responds with a significant counter-offer: 20%+ raise, promotion, new scope, retention bonus.

**Why this happens:** The current employer panics at losing a valued employee. Counter-offers are reactive, not proactive — they didn't value the person enough to act before they resigned.

**Response strategy:**

1. Don't panic. "I expected this might happen. Let's talk it through."
2. If you pre-seeded counter-offer doubt earlier, reference that conversation: "Remember when we talked about how you'd handle a counter? Here we are."
3. Ask what they offered specifically — understand the full counter-offer.
4. Reframe the counter: "If they could have given you this raise and promotion, why did it take your resignation? What does that tell you about how they value you proactively?"
5. Address the emotional pull: "Staying is comfortable. The people you know, the systems you understand. But the reasons you started looking haven't changed."
6. Share the data: "I've seen this play out many times. The research shows 50-80% of people who accept counter-offers leave within 18 months. The trust dynamics change — your manager knows you had one foot out the door."
7. Don't increase your offer reactively. If your offer was fair, stand behind it. Compete on the opportunity, not a bidding war.
8. Give them space: "This is a big decision. Take the weekend. I'm here if you want to talk through anything."

**Example language:**

- "I'm not surprised they countered. You're clearly valued. Here's what I'd ask you to consider: you started this process for reasons. A raise doesn't fix those reasons."
- "Counter-offers are flattering, and the money is real. But I've seen too many people accept them and find that 6 months later, the same frustrations are back — plus now their manager views them differently."
- "I'm not going to get into a bidding war. I believe our offer is fair and the opportunity is exceptional. What I want is for you to make the decision that's right for your career, not just your bank account this month."

**Mistakes to avoid:**

- Matching or exceeding the counter-offer in a panic — you'll set a precedent and the candidate will know they can extract more by threatening to leave.
- Being dismissive of the counter-offer. It's real money and a real temptation. Respect that.
- Badmouthing the current employer. Let the candidate draw their own conclusions.
- Issuing an ultimatum ("accept by tomorrow or we rescind"). Give reasonable space.

## 7. Candidate Asks for More Equity (Beyond Standard Grant)

**Situation:** Candidate says the equity component isn't enough. They want a larger grant, shorter cliff, or accelerated vesting.

**Why this happens:** Candidates who understand equity know it's where the real upside is. They may be comparing to a FAANG grant, have had a previous big win, or genuinely believe equity is the most important comp component.

**Response strategy:**

1. Validate the interest: "I love that you're thinking about equity — it means you're thinking long-term about the company."
2. Understand the specific ask: More shares? Shorter cliff? Faster vesting? Different structure?
3. Explain refresh grants if applicable: "Our initial grant is X, but we do annual refreshes of Y. Over 4 years, your total equity is actually Z."
4. If you can increase the grant: this is often easier to get approved than base increases because equity is forward-looking cost, not immediate cash.
5. If you can't increase the grant: explore vesting modifications (6-month cliff instead of 1 year, back-loaded to front-loaded).
6. For pre-IPO companies: help them understand the potential value. Use realistic scenarios, not pie-in-the-sky numbers.

**Example language:**

- "Let me walk you through the full equity picture including refreshes. The initial grant of [X] shares at [Y] price is just the starting point."
- "I have more flexibility on equity than I do on base. If I can increase the grant by [X]%, would that close the gap?"
- "What if we moved from a standard 4-year vest with 1-year cliff to a 4-year vest with 6-month cliff? That gets equity in your hands sooner."

**Mistakes to avoid:**

- Promising equity value outcomes. "These shares will be worth $X" is never something you should say. Use "at today's valuation" or "in this scenario" language.
- Not explaining the tax implications (ISOs vs NSOs, RSU taxation at vest, 83(b) elections). The candidate needs to understand what they're actually receiving.
- Ignoring internal equity — granting above-band equity to one hire creates problems when the team compares notes.

## 8. Candidate Wants Guaranteed Bonus (For First Year)

**Situation:** Candidate asks for their target bonus to be guaranteed for year 1 rather than performance-dependent. Common when they're leaving mid-year and forfeiting a bonus at their current employer.

**Why this happens:** Two reasons — either they're walking away from money at their current job (lost bonus, unvested equity), or they want a safety net since they can't influence a full year of performance metrics.

**Response strategy:**

1. This is one of the most reasonable asks a candidate can make. Treat it as such.
2. If they're forfeiting a current-year bonus: guarantee matches or partially offsets what they're losing. "You're leaving $40K on the table. Let me guarantee your bonus at target for year 1."
3. If they just want the safety net: consider a pro-rated guarantee based on start date. "Since you'll be starting in Q3, I'll guarantee your bonus at target for the stub period."
4. Structure it clearly: "Your target bonus is 15% of base. For your first calendar year, we'll guarantee that at 100% of target regardless of company performance factor."
5. If you can't guarantee the full bonus, offer a partial guarantee or a sign-on that equals the bonus amount.

**Example language:**

- "Completely fair. You can't control a full year of results before you've started. I'll guarantee your year-1 bonus at target."
- "We can guarantee the bonus at target for your first year. After that, it follows normal performance-based payout like everyone else."
- "I can't fully guarantee the bonus, but I can offer a $[X] sign-on that's roughly equivalent. Functionally the same for you, easier for me to get approved."

**Mistakes to avoid:**

- Saying no reflexively. This is one of the easiest concessions and it costs the company nothing if the person would have hit target anyway.
- Guaranteeing the bonus at above-target. Keep it at 100% target payout.
- Not clarifying the tax treatment. A guaranteed bonus is taxed as regular income.

## 9. Candidate Goes Silent After Verbal Offer (Ghosting Risk)

**Situation:** You delivered the verbal offer, the candidate seemed positive, and now they've gone radio silent. Emails and calls go unanswered for 3+ days.

**Why this happens:** They're weighing another offer, got a counter-offer, are dealing with personal circumstances, or have cold feet. Rarely is it malicious — silence usually means they're conflicted, not uninterested.

**Response strategy:**

1. Day 1-2 of silence: Normal. Don't panic. Send the written offer promptly and confirm receipt.
2. Day 3: One check-in via their preferred channel. "Hi [Name], I sent over the written offer details on [day]. Want to make sure you received everything. Happy to jump on a call if any questions came up."
3. Day 5: Try a different channel. If you emailed, try a LinkedIn message or text. Keep it casual and non-pressuring.
4. Day 7: Direct but warm. "I want to be respectful of your decision process. Can you give me a sense of your timeline? Even if you need more time, hearing from you helps me plan."
5. Day 10+: The breakup message. "I haven't heard back and I want to respect your time. I'm going to move forward with other candidates, but if your situation changes, I'd love to reconnect. No hard feelings."
6. Throughout: engage the hiring manager. A quick note from the HM ("excited to have you on the team, happy to answer questions") can break through when a recruiter message doesn't.

**Example language:**

- (Day 3): "Hey [Name], checking in on the offer we discussed. Any questions I can help with?"
- (Day 5): "I know this is a big decision. If something's giving you pause, I'd rather talk it through than have you stew on it alone."
- (Day 7): "I want to give you the space you need, but I also have stakeholders asking for an update. Can you let me know where your head is at, even if it's 'I need more time'?"
- (Day 10): "No response is a response, and I respect that. I'm going to keep the process moving, but if you want to revisit this, my door is open."

**Mistakes to avoid:**

- Flooding them with messages. Three unanswered messages in a row feels desperate.
- Getting passive-aggressive. "I've reached out several times..." — this guilt-trips and closes doors.
- Rescinding the offer prematurely without a breakup message. Always leave the door open.
- Not having a backup candidate warm. Silent candidates should trigger you to re-engage your #2.

## 10. Candidate Says "I Need to Talk to My Partner/Spouse"

**Situation:** Candidate says they need to discuss the offer with their partner before deciding. May be buying time, or may be genuinely involving their partner in a life decision.

**Why this happens:** Most of the time, it's genuine. Major career decisions affect families — relocation, comp changes, work-life balance shifts. Sometimes it's a polite way to buy time for another offer. Either way, the right response is the same.

**Response strategy:**

1. Respect it completely: "Absolutely. This is a decision that affects your whole household."
2. Offer to help: "Is there anything I can provide that would help the conversation? A total comp summary, benefits overview, information about the area if there's relocation involved?"
3. Gently identify the partner's likely concerns: "In my experience, partners often want to understand the stability of the company, the work-life balance, and the financial picture. I'm happy to address any of those."
4. Set a timeline: "Take the weekend. Can we reconnect Monday to talk through any questions that came up?"
5. If you suspect it's a stall: test with specificity. "Are there specific concerns your partner has that I could help address?" If the concerns are vague, they may be stalling.

**Example language:**

- "I totally get it. Big decisions are family decisions. What would be most helpful for me to put together for that conversation?"
- "If it would help, I can put together a one-page summary of the full package — comp, benefits, growth potential — that you can share at home."
- "Would it be helpful to connect your partner with someone who recently relocated their family to [city]? We have a few people who've been through it."

**Mistakes to avoid:**

- Pressuring them to decide without involving their partner. This creates resentment and can lead to a retracted acceptance.
- Assuming it's a stall tactic. Even if it is, treating it as genuine is the right move.
- Not providing materials to help the conversation. The partner only knows what the candidate tells them — make it easy to tell a compelling story.
- Forgetting to set a follow-up timeline. "Take your time" without a date leads to drift.

## 11. Candidate Wants Remote Work (Role Is Designated Hybrid/On-Site)

**Situation:** Candidate says they want fully remote or more remote days than the role allows. They may cite personal circumstances, a long commute, or simply a preference formed during the pandemic era.

**Why this happens:** Remote work has become a priority for many candidates, sometimes equal to or above comp. For some, it's a dealbreaker — for others, it's a strong preference they'll negotiate on if other factors are compelling.

**Response strategy:**

1. Understand the strength of the preference: "Is full remote a hard requirement, or is it a strong preference you'd consider flex on?"
2. If it's a hard requirement and the role can't accommodate it: be honest early. "I don't want to waste your time or ours. This role requires [X days] in office for [specific reason]. If that's a dealbreaker, I understand."
3. If there's flexibility: explore creative middle ground. "What if we started with 3 days in office for the first 90 days to build team relationships, then moved to 2 days? Would that work?"
4. Highlight what's non-negotiable and what's flexible. "The team meets Tuesday and Thursday in person. The other days are flexible."
5. If you lose the candidate on this: document it for the hiring manager. Aggregate data about candidates lost to remote-work policies informs future policy decisions.

**Example language:**

- "I hear you on remote. Let me check what flexibility we have. The key constraint is [specific reason for in-office requirement]."
- "Here's what I can offer: [X] days remote per week, plus full flexibility during school breaks and summer. Would that meet you partway?"
- "Several people on the team work a compressed schedule — 4 long days in office, Friday off. Would something like that appeal to you?"

**Mistakes to avoid:**

- Saying "we require on-site" without explaining why. Candidates accept constraints more readily when they understand the reason.
- Promising remote flexibility you can't deliver. If the HM expects butts in seats, don't negotiate away from that without their buy-in.
- Treating remote preference as a character flaw or lack of commitment. It's a legitimate preference.

## 12. Candidate Asks for Sign-On Bonus to Cover Lost Bonus

**Situation:** Candidate is leaving money on the table at their current employer — a bonus that's about to pay out, unvested RSUs, or a commission payout. They want to be "made whole."

**Why this happens:** This is a financial reality, not a negotiation tactic. The candidate is asking you to cover the cost of switching. If they stay 3 more months at their current job, they get $X. You're asking them to walk away from $X to join you.

**Response strategy:**

1. Take it seriously and get specific: "What exactly are you leaving on the table? Let me understand the timeline and amounts."
2. Verify if possible: "When does your bonus pay out? What's the unvested equity schedule?"
3. Structure the offset: Sign-on bonus is the standard vehicle. Match the amount they're forfeiting, or come close.
4. Add a clawback: "We'll provide a $[X] sign-on bonus. If you leave voluntarily within 12 months, you repay it pro-rata." This is standard and expected.
5. If the forfeited amount is very large: consider a structured payout. "We'll do $[X] at signing and $[Y] at your 6-month mark."

**Example language:**

- "I don't want you to take a financial hit to join us. Walk me through what you'd be leaving behind and let's figure out how to bridge it."
- "Your bonus pays out in March and you'd be giving up $[X]. I'll include a $[X] sign-on to make you whole. Standard 12-month clawback applies — if you leave in the first year, you repay it pro-rata."
- "Between the unvested RSUs and the annual bonus, you're looking at $[X] forfeited. Our sign-on will cover [Y%] of that. Combined with our equity grant vesting in year 1, you're actually ahead by month 14."

**Mistakes to avoid:**

- Dismissing the ask. They're making a real financial sacrifice.
- Not putting a clawback on the sign-on. Without it, a candidate could take the sign-on and leave in 6 months — you've paid them to interview.
- Offering the sign-on without understanding the full forfeiture picture. If they're losing $200K in unvested RSUs, a $20K sign-on doesn't solve the problem.
- Not accounting for tax gross-up. A $50K sign-on becomes ~$30K after taxes unless you gross it up.

## 13. Candidate Says "I'm Not in a Rush" (Slowing the Process)

**Situation:** Candidate explicitly says they're not in a hurry to make a decision. They may be waiting for other processes to play out, comfortable in their current role, or testing your urgency.

**Why this happens:** They have leverage and they know it. Or they're genuinely not desperate to move and want to make a thoughtful decision. Either way, they're setting the pace.

**Response strategy:**

1. Respect the pace while creating gentle urgency: "I respect that — a career move should be deliberate. Here's what I want to be transparent about on our side..."
2. Share legitimate business context: "The team is building for [specific initiative] and the hiring manager is eager to onboard someone by [date]. I don't want to pressure you, but I do want you to have full context."
3. Keep them engaged with value, not pressure: Share team updates, company news, invite them to meet additional team members.
4. Set milestones: "I'm not asking you to decide today. Can we agree on a check-in next [day]? I'll share any updates on my end, and you can share where your head is at."
5. If it goes too long (2+ weeks post-offer with no movement): honest conversation. "I want to keep this spot for you, but I have other candidates in process. Can you help me understand your timeline?"

**Example language:**

- "No rush at all. I do want to be upfront that we're in active conversations with other candidates, and I'd hate for timing to take this off the table for you."
- "I respect your pace. Let's set a check-in for Friday so I can give you any updates and we can see where things stand."
- "You mentioned you're not in a rush. Can I ask what the ideal timeline looks like from your side? That helps me manage things internally."

**Mistakes to avoid:**

- Creating artificial urgency they can see through. "We have 3 other candidates about to accept" — if it's not true, don't say it.
- Letting the process drift indefinitely. Two weeks post-offer is a reasonable limit before a direct conversation.
- Showing desperation. If you act like they're the only candidate, they'll negotiate harder.
- Pulling the offer without warning. Always give a heads-up before any deadline.

## 14. Multiple Candidates — Dual-Close Scenario

**Situation:** You have two strong finalists. Both are in the offer stage or close to it. You need to close one without losing the other as a backup, and you need to manage timing carefully.

**Why this happens:** Good sourcing and a strong process sometimes delivers two excellent candidates simultaneously. This is a high-class problem, but it's still a problem — mishandling it means losing both.

**Response strategy:**

1. Rank them: The HM must choose a #1 and #2. No "we like both equally" — that's a decision avoidance, not a strategy.
2. Extend to #1 first with a defined timeline: "We'd like to extend an offer. I'll send written details today and would love your decision by [date, 5-7 business days]."
3. Keep #2 warm without lying: "You're a very strong candidate. We're in final stages of decision-making and I expect to have an update for you by [date]." Do not say "you're our second choice."
4. If #1 is negotiating and taking time: you can use the timeline honestly. "I want to give you time, and I also have other candidates I owe a timely answer to. Can we aim for [date]?"
5. If #1 declines: move to #2 immediately. The offer should feel first-choice, not consolation. "We've been really impressed throughout this process. I'd like to extend an offer..."
6. If #1 accepts: let #2 down gracefully and keep the relationship warm for future roles.

**Example language:**

- (To #2 while #1 is deciding): "You're in a strong position. I'm working through some logistics on our end and I'll have a definitive update for you by [date]. I appreciate your patience."
- (If #1 declines, to #2): "I'm excited to share that we'd like to extend an offer. The team was genuinely impressed with [specific thing], and [HM name] is particularly excited about [specific area]."
- (If #1 accepts, to #2): "I want to be straight with you — we've made an offer to another candidate who accepted. This isn't a reflection of you — you were exceptional. I'd love to keep in touch for [future role / next opening]."

**Mistakes to avoid:**

- Extending offers to both simultaneously. If both accept, you're in a terrible spot.
- Keeping #2 waiting too long. More than 7-10 business days of "we'll get back to you" and they'll move on or feel disrespected.
- Letting #2 find out they were second choice. Frame the eventual offer (if #1 declines) as a straightforward "we want you," not "the other person said no."
- Not having the #1/#2 conversation with the HM. If you don't force the rank, you'll be stuck when you need to move fast.
