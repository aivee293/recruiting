# Compensation Packaging Guide

Frameworks for calculating, presenting, and explaining total compensation packages. Covers total comp math, equity explanations, offer letter patterns, benefits valuation, and internal equity considerations.

---

## 1. Total Comp Calculator Framework

### Year 1 Total Compensation

The number that matters most to candidates making a switch decision:

```
Year 1 Total = Base Salary
             + Sign-On Bonus
             + Target Bonus (or Guaranteed Bonus if applicable)
             + Year 1 Equity Value
             + Relocation (if applicable)
```

**Notes:**
- Use target bonus (e.g., 15% of base at 100% payout), not max or stretch
- For equity, use the value of shares vesting in year 1 only (often $0 if there's a 1-year cliff)
- Sign-on is one-time — always separate it from recurring comp when comparing offers
- Include relocation only if the candidate is relocating; it's not comp, it's cost offset

### Annualized Total Compensation

The steady-state number for year 2 and beyond — what the candidate earns annually once sign-on and ramp-up effects are gone:

```
Annualized Total = Base Salary
                 + Target Bonus
                 + Annual Equity Vest (grant / vesting years)
```

**Notes:**
- No sign-on (one-time)
- No relocation (one-time)
- Equity assumes standard equal vesting (4-year grant / 4 = annual vest value)
- If vesting is back-loaded (e.g., Amazon's 5/15/40/40), show each year separately

### 4-Year Total Compensation

For equity-heavy packages, the 4-year view shows the full grant value:

```
4-Year Total = (Base Salary x 4)
             + (Target Bonus x 4)
             + Full Equity Grant Value
             + Sign-On Bonus
             + Relocation
```

**Notes:**
- Assumes base and bonus stay flat (conservative)
- Does not include equity refreshes (which can be substantial at larger companies)
- Useful for comparing packages with different equity structures (front-loaded vs back-loaded)

### Equity Valuation by Company Stage

| Company Stage | How to Value Equity | Confidence Level | What to Tell Candidates |
|---------------|-------------------|-----------------|------------------------|
| **Public** | Current stock price x shares | High | "At today's price of $X, your grant is worth $Y. Stock price can go up or down." |
| **Late-stage private (Series C+)** | Last preferred price x shares (apply 20-40% discount for illiquidity) | Medium | "Based on our last funding round, the estimated value is $Y. This is pre-liquidity, so there's both upside potential and risk." |
| **Early-stage (Seed-Series B)** | Strike price for options, acknowledge high uncertainty | Low | "The strike price is $X. The potential value depends on company growth and a future liquidity event. I can walk you through scenarios." |
| **Pre-revenue** | Percentage of company, scenario-based | Very Low | "You'd own X% of the company. Here are three scenarios for what that could look like at different exit valuations." |

**Rule:** Never promise equity value. Always use "at today's valuation" or "in this scenario" language. For pre-IPO companies, present multiple scenarios (base, bull, bear) and be explicit about risk.

## 2. Equity Explanation Templates

### RSUs at a Public Company

> "We're granting you [X] restricted stock units (RSUs). Each RSU converts to one share of [Company] stock when it vests. At today's stock price of $[Y], your total grant is worth approximately $[Z].
>
> **Vesting schedule:** The standard schedule is 4 years with a 1-year cliff. That means nothing vests in the first 12 months. At your 1-year anniversary, 25% of your grant (X shares) vests all at once. After that, the remaining 75% vests quarterly (or monthly) over the next 3 years.
>
> **Taxes:** When RSUs vest, they're taxed as ordinary income based on the stock price that day. Most people choose to 'sell to cover' — the company automatically sells enough shares to cover your tax bill, and you keep the rest.
>
> **Refreshes:** Each year, you're eligible for an additional equity grant (a 'refresh') based on your performance and level. This means your total equity holdings grow over time, not just from the initial grant."

### RSUs at a Pre-IPO Company

> "We're granting you [X] RSUs. These work similarly to public-company RSUs, but there are some important differences.
>
> **Valuation:** Since we're not yet public, the value is based on our most recent 409A valuation (the IRS-required independent valuation), which prices shares at $[Y]. Based on our last funding round at $[Z] per share, the implied value of your grant is approximately $[total].
>
> **Vesting:** Same 4-year schedule with a 1-year cliff. Shares vest on schedule, but you can't sell them until a liquidity event — IPO, acquisition, or a secondary sale window.
>
> **Liquidity:** We don't have a guaranteed liquidity timeline. What I can tell you is [relevant context: IPO plans, secondary market availability, company trajectory]. The shares represent real ownership with real upside potential, but they're not liquid today.
>
> **Taxes:** RSUs at a private company can trigger a tax event at vesting (you owe taxes on the value, even though you can't sell). Some companies offer early exercise or other structures to manage this. I can connect you with our equity team or a tax advisor if you'd like to understand the implications."

### Stock Options (ISOs vs NSOs) at a Startup

> "We're granting you [X] stock options with a strike price of $[Y] per share. Options give you the right to buy shares at that price — your upside is the difference between the strike price and what the shares are worth at exit.
>
> **ISO vs NSO:** Your grant is [ISOs/NSOs/a mix].
> - **ISOs (Incentive Stock Options):** Tax-advantaged. No ordinary income tax when you exercise (if you hold the shares for 1+ year after exercise and 2+ years after grant). Gains taxed as long-term capital gains. However, the 'spread' at exercise can trigger AMT (Alternative Minimum Tax). ISOs are only available to employees and capped at $100K/year in vesting value.
> - **NSOs (Non-Qualified Stock Options):** The spread at exercise is taxed as ordinary income, regardless of how long you hold. Available to employees, contractors, and advisors. No AMT concern, but higher tax rate.
>
> **Vesting:** 4-year vest with a 1-year cliff. After the cliff, options vest monthly. You have the right to exercise vested options at any time (subject to company policy).
>
> **Exercise window:** If you leave the company, you typically have [90 days / X months / post-termination exercise period] to exercise your vested options. Some companies offer extended exercise windows — ours is [X].
>
> **Scenario math:**
> - Strike price: $[Y] per share
> - If shares are worth $[Z] at exit: your profit per share is $[Z - Y]
> - Your [X] options would be worth $[(Z - Y) x X] before taxes
>
> I'd recommend talking to a tax advisor about exercise timing strategy, especially for ISOs."

### Phantom Stock / Profit Sharing

> "Instead of traditional equity, we offer [phantom stock / profit sharing units]. These give you the economic benefit of ownership without actual shares.
>
> **How it works:** You receive [X] phantom units. Each unit tracks the value of one share. When a liquidity event occurs (sale, IPO, or defined trigger), you receive a cash payout equal to the appreciation in value of your units.
>
> **Key differences from real equity:** You don't own actual shares, so there's no voting rights or cap table presence. The benefit is simplicity — no exercise decisions, no stock certificates, no 83(b) elections. The tax treatment is straightforward: it's taxed as ordinary income when paid out.
>
> **Vesting:** Same 4-year schedule. The units vest over time, and your payout at a liquidity event is based on the number of vested units."

### Explaining Vesting Schedules Simply

Use this framework when candidates ask "how does vesting work?"

> "Think of it like this: the company is giving you [X] shares, but they're delivered over time to ensure we're both committed to the relationship.
>
> **The cliff:** For the first 12 months, nothing vests. This is the cliff period. If either of us decides it's not a fit in the first year, neither side has made a big equity commitment. On your 1-year anniversary, 25% of your total grant vests all at once.
>
> **After the cliff:** The remaining 75% vests in equal installments — usually monthly or quarterly — over the next 3 years. By your 4-year anniversary, you own 100% of the grant.
>
> **If you leave:** You keep everything that's already vested. Unvested shares go back to the company. With options, you'll have a window (usually 90 days) to decide whether to exercise your vested options."

### Addressing "What's It Worth?"

| Candidate asks... | Response framework |
|-------------------|-------------------|
| "What will the stock be worth?" | "I can't predict the future stock price. What I can show you is the current value and historical trajectory." (Public company) |
| "When will there be a liquidity event?" | "I don't have a guaranteed date. Here's what I can share about the company's trajectory and stated plans." (Private company) |
| "How does this compare to [FAANG grant]?" | "Different risk/reward profile. Their grant is liquid and predictable. Ours has more upside potential but less certainty. Let me walk you through the scenarios." |
| "Can I sell my shares?" | Public: "Yes, after vesting, subject to trading windows." Private: "Not until a liquidity event, unless we open a secondary sale window." |
| "What if the company goes under?" | Options: "Your options would be worthless — you wouldn't exercise them." RSUs: "Unvested RSUs are lost. Vested RSUs at a private company... it depends on the outcome." |

## 3. Offer Letter Language Patterns

Key clauses and how to present them to candidates who ask questions.

### At-Will Employment

**Standard language:**
> "Your employment with [Company] is at-will, meaning either you or the Company may terminate the employment relationship at any time, with or without cause and with or without notice."

**How to present it:**
> "This is standard at-will language — it's in virtually every US offer letter. It means neither of us is locked in. You can leave any time, and we can part ways any time. In practice, we have a [notice period expectation / separation process] that applies."

### Equity Grant Language

**Standard language:**
> "Subject to approval by the Company's Board of Directors, you will be granted [X] [RSUs / stock options] under the Company's [Year] Equity Incentive Plan. The grant will be subject to the terms and conditions of the Plan and applicable grant agreement."

**How to present it:**
> "The 'subject to board approval' language is standard corporate governance — the board needs to formally approve all equity grants. This is a formality in our case; I've never seen a board reject an approved hire's equity grant. You'll receive the formal grant agreement after your start date, which will detail the vesting schedule, exercise terms (if options), and other specifics."

### Bonus Language (Target vs Guaranteed)

**Target bonus:**
> "You will be eligible for an annual performance bonus with a target of [X]% of your base salary. Actual payout is based on individual and company performance, and is subject to the terms of the Company's bonus plan."

**Guaranteed bonus:**
> "For your first calendar year of employment, your annual bonus of [X]% of base salary will be guaranteed at 100% of target, payable at the same time as standard bonus payouts. For subsequent years, your bonus will be performance-based per the Company's bonus plan."

**How to present the difference:**
> "Target bonus means if you and the company both hit goals, you earn $[X]. It could be higher (if performance exceeds targets) or lower (if targets are missed). Guaranteed bonus for year 1 means you get $[X] regardless of performance — it removes the uncertainty while you're ramping up."

### Clawback Provisions (Sign-On, Relocation)

**Standard language:**
> "The sign-on bonus of $[X] is subject to repayment if you voluntarily resign or are terminated for cause within [12/24] months of your start date. Repayment will be pro-rated based on months of employment completed."

**How to present it:**
> "The clawback is standard for sign-on bonuses. If you stay 12 months, you keep everything. If you leave at 6 months, you'd repay half. It's a pro-rated structure — the longer you stay, the less you'd owe. We include this because the sign-on is an investment in the relationship."

**Relocation clawback:**
> "Similar to the sign-on, relocation benefits of $[X] are subject to pro-rated repayment if you voluntarily leave within [12/24] months. This is standard for managed relocations."

### Start Date Contingencies

**Standard language:**
> "This offer is contingent upon satisfactory completion of a background check, verification of your right to work in [country], and execution of the Company's standard employee agreements (including confidentiality and IP assignment)."

**How to present it:**
> "Standard contingencies — background check, right-to-work verification, and signing our standard employee agreements. The background check typically takes [X] business days. The employee agreements cover confidentiality and IP assignment — I can send you a copy to review before your start date if you'd like."

### Background Check / Reference Language

**Standard language:**
> "This offer is contingent upon the successful completion of a background check conducted by [Provider]. The background check may include verification of education, employment history, criminal record check, and [credit check / other]."

**How to present it:**
> "The background check verifies the basics — employment history, education, criminal record. It's standard for all hires. If anything comes up that doesn't match what you've shared, we'll give you a chance to explain before making any decisions. Is there anything you'd want to flag proactively?"

## 4. Benefits Valuation

How to quantify the value of benefits and present them as part of the total rewards picture.

### Employer Health Insurance Contribution

```
Annual value = Employer monthly premium contribution x 12
```

| Coverage Type | Typical Employer Annual Contribution (US) |
|--------------|------------------------------------------|
| Employee only | $6,000 - $9,000 |
| Employee + spouse | $12,000 - $16,000 |
| Family | $16,000 - $24,000 |

**How to present:** "Our health insurance is covered at [X]% by the company. For your coverage type, that's approximately $[Y] per year in employer-paid premiums — real money you'd be paying out of pocket elsewhere."

### Retirement Matching

```
Annual value = Salary x Match percentage (up to cap)
```

| Match Structure | Example | Annual Value (on $150K salary) |
|----------------|---------|-------------------------------|
| 50% match up to 6% of salary | Company matches $0.50 per $1.00 on first 6% | $4,500 |
| 100% match up to 4% of salary | Company matches $1.00 per $1.00 on first 4% | $6,000 |
| 100% match up to 6% of salary | Company matches $1.00 per $1.00 on first 6% | $9,000 |

**How to present:** "Our 401(k) match is [structure]. If you contribute [X]%, the company adds $[Y] per year. That's free money — and it compounds over time."

### PTO Valuation

```
Daily rate = Base salary / 260 (working days)
PTO value = Daily rate x PTO days
Comparative value = Daily rate x (Your PTO days - Their PTO days)
```

**Example:** $150K salary, 25 PTO days vs competitor offering 15 PTO days:
- Daily rate: $577
- 10 extra days = $5,770 in equivalent value

**How to present:** "We offer [X] days of PTO. If you compare that to a company offering [Y] days, those [X-Y] extra days are worth approximately $[Z] in salary equivalent. Or think of it as [X-Y] more days with your family."

### Learning & Development Budget

| Benefit | Typical Range | How to Value |
|---------|--------------|-------------|
| Conference budget | $2,000 - $5,000/year | Direct dollar value |
| Learning stipend | $1,000 - $5,000/year | Direct dollar value |
| Tuition reimbursement | $5,250 - $15,000/year | Tax-advantaged up to $5,250 |
| Certification budget | $500 - $3,000/year | Direct dollar value + career ROI |

### Other Perks

| Perk | Typical Value | How to Present |
|------|--------------|---------------|
| Home office stipend | $500 - $2,000 (one-time) | "We'll set you up with everything you need" |
| Wellness stipend | $500 - $2,000/year | "Gym, mental health, whatever supports your wellbeing" |

| Meal benefits | $1,000 - $5,000/year | Highlight if on-site/hybrid |
| Parental leave | Varies widely | Weeks x weekly salary equivalent |
| Sabbatical | Varies | Highlight as differentiator if offered |

## 5. Internal Equity Considerations

### New Hire Above Existing Team Members

**When it happens:** Market has moved faster than internal comp adjustments. The new hire's market rate exceeds what current employees at the same level are earning.

**How to handle:**
1. **Surface it early.** Before extending the offer, check the comp of existing team members at the same level. If the new hire would come in above them, flag it to the hiring manager and HR/comp team.
2. **Quantify the gap.** "The new hire's offer is $X. We have 3 people at the same level earning $Y-Z. The gap is [percentage]."
3. **Propose a remediation plan.** Options include:
   - Market adjustment for existing employees (ideal, but expensive)
   - Flagging for next comp cycle with priority adjustment
   - Equity or bonus differential to partially close the gap
   - Accepting the gap with a plan to address it within 6-12 months
4. **Document the decision.** Whatever the choice, record why the gap exists and the plan to address it. This protects the company if it becomes an issue later.

**What NOT to do:**
- Lowball the new hire to maintain internal equity. You'll lose the candidate and the role stays open longer, which costs more.
- Hire above band and hope nobody notices. Compensation data travels faster than you think.
- Promise the HM you'll fix it without HR/comp buy-in for a budget adjustment.

### Band Compression

**What it is:** When market salaries rise faster than internal adjustments, the gap between junior and senior employees narrows. A Senior Engineer hired 3 years ago might earn the same as a mid-level engineer hired today.

**How to identify it:**
- Plot team compensation against levels on a scatter chart
- Look for cases where Level N employees earn within 5% of Level N+1 employees
- Check new hire offers against the median of existing employees at the same level

**How to address it:**
1. **Proactive:** Build annual market adjustment budgets separate from merit increases. Merit rewards performance; market adjustments maintain competitiveness.
2. **Reactive:** When you see compression, build a business case. "We have X employees who are Y% below market. The cost to adjust is $Z. The cost to replace them if they leave is $[Z x 1.5-2x]."
3. **In the offer context:** If you're about to hire someone who creates compression, be transparent with the HM. "This offer is market-rate for the new hire. It will create compression with [names/count]. Here's my recommendation for addressing it."

### When to Flag a Comp Equity Concern

| Situation | Action | Who to Flag |
|-----------|--------|-------------|
| New hire within 5% of next level up | Flag before extending offer | Hiring Manager + Comp/HR |
| New hire 10%+ above same-level peers | Require remediation plan before offer | Hiring Manager + Comp/HR + Finance |
| Two employees at same level with 15%+ comp gap | Investigate root cause, propose adjustment | Comp/HR |
| Promotion would result in lower comp than new hires at that level | Adjust promotion comp to at least match | Hiring Manager + Comp/HR |
| Candidate negotiations push offer into next level's band | Re-evaluate level or cap the offer | Hiring Manager + Comp/HR |

**The conversation with the hiring manager:**
> "I want to flag something before we extend this offer. [Candidate] needs $[X] to accept, which is [Y]% above [Employee Name] who's been at this level for [Z] years. I have three options: (1) adjust [Employee]'s comp now at a cost of $[A], (2) flag [Employee] for priority adjustment at next cycle, or (3) accept the gap and monitor. My recommendation is [option] because [reasoning]. What are your thoughts?"

**Key principle:** Internal equity problems don't go away — they compound. The cost of addressing them grows over time. Surface them early, address them proactively, and document the decisions made.
